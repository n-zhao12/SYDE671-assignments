2.1 · Image sharpening

Unsharp masking in one convolution

A Gaussian blur retains broad structure while attenuating high frequencies. Subtracting the blur from the original isolates detail; adding a scaled version of that detail back strengthens local contrast. Combining the operations gives a symmetric unsharp kernel, applied independently to the RGB channels.

Isharp = I + α(I − G ∗ I) = [(1 + α)δ − αG] ∗ I

Here, δ is the 2D impulse (identity) filter, so convolving it with an image leaves the image unchanged; σ = 3 pixels is the standard deviation of the Gaussian blur G. α is the unsharp amount shown in the figures; it controls sharpening strength by setting how much high-frequency detail is added back. An amount of 0 leaves the image unchanged; larger amounts make existing detail more pronounced and can create halos. I compare α = 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 with a Gaussian standard deviation of 3 pixels. The explicit kernel is 25 × 25, extending four standard deviations in each direction. Reflect-101 boundary handling avoids introducing a black exterior into these photographs. Because the kernel is symmetric, OpenCV's correlation operation gives the same result as convolution.

Can sharpening undo blur?

For the evaluation, I start with the additional sharp photograph and blur it with a Gaussian whose standard deviation is σ = 3 pixels, then sharpen that blurred version at α = 1.5. The re-sharpened result has stronger edges than the blurred image, but it does not recover the original fine detail. Unsharp masking amplifies what remains; it is not an inverse of the blur.

2.2 · Hybrid images

Two identities, separated by frequency

A hybrid adds a low-pass version of image A to a high-pass version of image B. Fine detail from B is more prominent nearby, whereas the broad appearance of A is intended to dominate at a distance or smaller display scale. The balance depends on alignment, filter scale, and source contrast.

H = Gσlow ∗ A + (B − Gσhigh ∗ B)

Full process demonstration: Derek + Nutmeg

Derek supplies the low frequencies and Nutmeg supplies the high frequencies. I align their eye landmarks with a similarity transform: translation, uniform scale, and rotation. A softened support mask attenuates high-pass content outside the warped image area.

Cutoff-frequency selection

The selected scales are σlow = 8 pixels and σhigh = 5 pixels at the loaded image resolution. These are smooth Gaussian roll-offs, not hard frequency cutoffs. I compare low-pass sigmas 4, 8, and 12 while holding the high-pass setting fixed, then high-pass sigmas 2.5, 5, and 7.5 while holding the low-pass setting fixed.

2.3 · Gaussian and Laplacian stacks

Five scales, one image size

A stack keeps every level at the original image size, unlike a pyramid that downsamples. These stacks contain the original followed by Gaussian levels with effective standard deviations 2, 4, 8, and 16 pixels. No built-in pyramid functions are used.

Each Laplacian band is the difference between adjacent Gaussian levels. The last band is the coarsest Gaussian residual. Together, the bands represent the original image.

Li = Gi − Gi+1 for i = 0…3;   L4 = G4;   I = ∑i=04 Li

2.4 · Multiresolution blending

A different seam width for each frequency

A hard cut changes abruptly at every frequency. A single broad feather can mix fine texture across too wide a region. Instead, I build a Gaussian stack of the mask and use its matching level to weight each Laplacian band.

Ci = MiLA,i + (1 − Mi)LB,i;   C = ∑i=04 Ci

Mask value 1 selects A and 0 selects B. Blurring the mask makes its transition wider at coarser levels: fine texture changes near the seam, while broad color and illumination blend over a wider area.
